The image above is of Lake Ontario. This is a view I see when I take walks along a long pathway between where I live and downtown Toronto. In many places in Ontario and New York State, you can see a view of the lake just like this one.

This lake is one of the Great Lakes, of which there are five: Superior, Huron, Michigan, Erie, and Ontario. If you live in Ontario or some northern US states, these lakes are quite familiar. They are large, and have been a source of recreation, shipping, and fishing for a long time.

In fact, these lakes have been around for longer than either the US or Canada has been in existence. These lakes have been known to people like the Wendat and Anishinaabe for centuries, likely even centuries before the French and English arrived. The modern names are based on the original names Indigenous groups have given these lakes a long time ago. For example, Lake Ontario comes from the Ojibwe *Ontari’io*, translating roughly to "large lake/beautiful lake". Lake Superior was known as *gichi-gami* in Ojibwe, which roughly translates to "large sea". The names of the Great Lakes basically denote that they are, in fact, lakes that are great. It follows that regions around these lakes such as the US state of Michigan or the province of Ontario take their names from the lakes. The lakes came first before the settlers of Canada and the US.

From a human perspective, the Great Lakes are very old. People have been gathered around these bodies of water for over a thousand years. They have been here a long, *long* time.

On a shorter scale, Canada and the US are currently in the midst of a trade war, with President Trump [imposing tariffs on Canadian goods](https://www.cbc.ca/news/world/trump-canada-us-trade-tariffs-cusma-usmca-9.7276973) and Canada responding with [counter tariffs](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/08/list-of-products-from-the-united-states-subject-to-counter-tariffs-effective-september-8-2026.html). I won't get into the details of why this dispute is happening, and any opinions I have about this moment in Canada-US relations. The only notable aspect of this is that as a "response" to Canada, the US President ordered the [renaming of Lake Ontario to Lake America](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/27/politics/trump-canada-lake-ontario-america). This caused a lot of commotion and commentary, with nearly uniform negative response from people in Ontario and Canada. In conversations with loved ones, we discussed how would this even work? Can one of the Great Lakes be renamed?

It turns out that that the answer is a partial yes. On the US side of things, Trump ordered a change from the [US Board on Geographic Names](https://www.usgs.gov/us-board-on-geographic-names) to rename Lake Ontario to Lake America on all US maps and communications. The Board of Geographic Names complied with this order and went about renaming the lake. (It should be noted that since Lake Ontario has a maritime border between the US and Canada, only US documents would have the new name, while Canadian documents would retain the original name.).

Downstream of these government changes to the name are mapping services such as Google Maps. Google Maps is likely the largest mapping service in the world right now, serving updated maps to consumers and institutions all over the world. At one point Google Maps employed over one thousand people, all maintaining all the systems that add up to Google Maps. It is no trivial service or small piece of software. People all over the world use Google Maps for everything from driving directions to surveys of urban areas and beyond. Google Maps is a remarkable piece of technology, spanning all area of the globe in a literal sense.

One key piece of infrastructure for Google Maps and other mapping services are government agencies like the US Board of Geographic Names. These are supposed to be knowledgeable bodies that provide up-to-date geographic information including place names from nations all over the world. Geographic names are updated all the time for a variety of reasons, some of which are highly political. For example, India and China have been embroiled in a [territorial dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Indian_border_dispute) for some time along their land border. Nations such as South Sudan are created, requiring new geographic borders on maps. And sometimes useless authoritarians [like to rename things for no reason](https://theconversation.com/yes-trump-can-rename-the-gulf-of-mexico-just-not-for-everyone-heres-how-it-works-248009). While there is much to discuss in some of these boundary conflicts, eventually lines need to be actually drawn - on maps.

Google Maps needs to draw a map one way or the other. And since Google Maps is digital and is essentially a service that is always available and up-to-date, decisions need to be made about which names are assigned to which places. Mapmakers are aware of the political nature of naming things, and try to put in place processes that provide accuracy while maintaining as much political neutrality as possible. For example, maps of the China-India border may look different in Google Maps based on whether you're viewing the map from China or India. Both countries may deliver official maps and place names to Google Maps, and Google Maps may then process these places as needed. Google Maps is not the final arbiter in this process, and it has good reason to avoid being so. 

The same may go for re-naming Lake Ontario to Lake America. If the US government has the right to "officially" rename this lake, and goes through the institutional motions to do so, then Google Maps (and other map services) may oblige and apply the renaming. Just like in other places in the world, Google Maps by design does not decide on the place names even if the rename is, bluntly, ridiculously stupid.

Some people have interpreted Google Maps decision to show Lake Ontario as Lake America as "bending the knee" to an authoritarian and Google is "taking sides" in this matter. At the same time, other mapping services have gotten [increased attention and support](https://www.kxan.com/news/mapquest-tops-apple-app-store-charts-after-refusing-trumps-lake-america-change/) for not updating the name of Lake Ontario. While I do think this is a good thing, I think the teams at Google Maps are standing by a decision made a while ago in service of their goal to provide the most up-to-date mapping information available. They do not arbitrate mapping decisions. As someone who's worked in software for over a decade now, I can see the benefit of this approach. 

Making a decision and standing by it can be tough for many organizations, and software can be particularly pernicious in some cases. Changing a few lines of code and pushing an update can be done in minutes, and often in software organizations there's no real hard consequences for changing a process. Google Maps could ignore this geographic change for just one time. However, this would have some impact on Google Maps' mission. If other contentious conflicts can simply be ignored, why not ignore those ones too? The problem is that this starts to look like Google Maps is making decisions, which compromises their reputation. As well, a tool that is supposed to be as politically neutral as possible steps away from that position. This may ultimately seen as a sin of omission, a small chip off Google Maps' product quality that might create some larger cracks later on. Even if you are someone far removed from the Great Lakes region, or even Canada or the US, you may start to question Google Maps mapping just a little bit more. Ultimately this could be bad sign for a sterling product: facing a moral, existential threat from above and slow but growing displeasure from below. In the world of software, there's been many instance of a once dominant and superior software product ending up as an also-ran competitor. As useful and powerful as Google Maps is currently, this may not always be the case.

Software has only been in existance for a fraction of the time humans have been on Earth, and humans in turn have only been around for a fraction of the time the Earth has existed. 

The Great Lakes, on the other hand, have been around long before people came to them, and will be around long after people leave their shores.
