Lately I've been working with a browser-based test framework. It's the kind of thing I have the most experience with and generally enjoy. When I think about what makes a test framework like this good, I obviously thought about the Centre Pompidou in Paris.

The [Centre Pompidou](https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/) is a large building in Paris, housing some libraries and the National Art Modern Art Museum. It was built in the 1970s and completed in 1977. Notably, the Centre Pompidou was designed to be an ["inside-out"](https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/pompidou-plus/magazine/article/architectural-secrets-the-piazza-of-the-centre-pompidou#&gid=lightbox-group-37599&pid=0) building, where infrastructure such as heating, water, and electrical was run on the exterior of the building instead of the more typical interior. As a result, the centre has pipes in various colours running along its exterior containing water, wires, and so on. Many architectural critics hated this design, thinking it was a unnecessary and ugly for a building to expose its infrastructure. On the other hand, some praised the architects for taking a new perspective of a public building, exposing the "guts" of a large facility for public view and education.

To me, this kind of architectural design makes a lot of sense to apply to test frameworks.

In many cases, as test frameworks grow in terms of test or line count, they also grow in complexity. More tests means handling more cases, and often times, handling many scenarios that are similar but not exactly the same. Think of entering a valid name, an invalid name, a blank name, and name with special characters into a text field. These scenarios might be able to use the same test code logic. They may also not be able to; a blank input might have slightly different errors or UX than an invalid name. A test framework that might easily handle an invalid name case might not be able to handle a blank name case. This is a simple example; as test frameworks grow, there could be many scenarios like this that look like they should "just work" but actually require some rethinking and plumbing of test logic to make things work well. 

On top of this, test code often needs to live for a long time while being straightforward to refactor. Tests run often, and the expectation is that test code complies and runs even more regularly than app code. Concepts like DRY might not help long-term with maintenance of test code, due to similar but different scenarios like above. It's often better to have [damp test code](https://www.tumblr.com/simplythetest/93114654620/keep-your-test-code-damp) than completely dry. Being able to make small adjustments or even avoid complicated refactorings is important.

This is where exposing your infrastructure comes in. One of the benefits of buildings like the Centre Pompidou is that maintenance teams can make infrastructural changes on the outside of the building without disturbing the inside. Museum visitors and artists can walk around viewing or installing art without being disrupted by electrical work or updating water pipes. As well, it's easy for both casual viewers and maintenance workers to see where exactly what infrastructural work may be needed. 

Some software architectures may look ugly at first, but beauty in the eye of the beholder, and often the developer who's responsible for them.
