Sure, art is fantastic, and one of the wonderful parts of being a human. But what about art galleries?

I recently read this great piece on the [legacy of artist Michael Asher](https://hyperallergic.com/for-michael-asher-the-museum-was-the-medium/). Asher is thought of as the creator of a form of conceptual art called institutional critique. This form of concept art focuses on highlighting aspects of institutions such as museums and galleries that are often overlooked or played down. The main idea here is to create pieces that get people thinking about how institutions work and show the good, bad, and ugly of such institutions.

For example, the author of the article on Asher, Renée Reizman, details one of his works where he created a book in a style close to the Museum of Modern Art's design style listing art pieces that the MoMA had de-accessioned, or gotten rid of through sales or otherwise. Adding to collections is something that many people think about when they think of art galleries, but removing items from collections is not. In fact, some people may not enjoy finding out their favourite galleries no longer have some of their favourite art pieces.

Now, I really enjoy [conceptual](https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/2008.232/) [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/America_(Cattelan)), even when I don't particularly [like it](https://publicdelivery.org/jenny-holzer-lustmord/#lustmord). Mainly I like that conceptual art can be both minimalistic and cerebral; I think good concept art works overtime to make you think. Institutional critique narrows its focus on, well, institutions, and makes you think about them. The "critique" part can mean pointing out lesser known or unpopular aspects of institutions, but it can also shed light on how institutions work overall.

This brings me to art galleries. It took me until around 40 to really understand that I like art galleries - from prestigious to humble - and _why_ exactly I like art galleries. First, art galleries are a place you can nearly always go to. If they're open, you can go in (sometimes for a fee). You can walk around, stay in one place or venture all around. You can look at some art, all art, or no art. You can rest. Art galleries are genuine third-spaces and in some cases, excellent ones. Second, art galleries have cool stuff. Cool stuff is helpful for having good ideas and getting inspired. And lastly, like art itself, art galleries provide an opportunity to go somewhere. Galleries give you an excuse to travel. The Mona Lisa attracts millions of visitors a year, no matter what people think of it.

In some ways, Asher's work is a deep love letter to art galleries. Something Reizman notes in the exhibit is that all the descriptions of the pieces are found in a single book located within the gallery and not on cards near each individual art piece. While Reizman thinks this format is questionable, I think there's another intention. Galleries often place descriptions next to art pieces or at least nearby. This has the benefit of letting viewers see the art _and optionally_ get additional information at the same time. If you're interested in a piece of art, you can get more information conveniently. If not, no bother, move along. Having small cards along with art is almost a default approach namely because it's a convenience to viewers. Having a single book instead provides a possible alternative that is, well, not as good. This is a "critique" that shows a benefit, not a cost.

Art galleries provide several benefits like this. Curated exhibits and gallery halls is a benefit, showing art in contexts like chronological or conceptual similarity. Artwork can be staged appropriately with good conditions. And, mercifully, there's often somewhere to get a cup of coffee or something stronger. Galleries provide these things. Even if there's an expectation that some or all of these aspects are provided, perhaps even taken for granted, it can be helpful to remind people that these are good things.
