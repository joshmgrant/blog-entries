Personally, I think it's time for a revolution. 

A revolution in the quality of software. 

I've been thinking about software quality, in terms of the agile quadrants for a while. In particular, since I come from a test automation/programming background, so I feel like I have a lot to learn about the "first quadrant" on the upper right. This area addresses, roughly, product quality. Questions such as "Do end users like using this software?", "Does this software solve a real problem for customers?", or "Is this new feature buggy?" are of interest in this aspect of software quality. In one way, this area of software quality is what many people think of when they think of software quality and testing ("Find bugs and report them"-style approaches), but in another, it is so, _so_ much more.

Two recent stories related to software have made me think that, generally speaking, product quality is not great in the software world.

The first involves the Olympics, Benson Boone, and Bananas. The second involves a high ranking tech director losing her emails.

During the Winter Olympics this year (congratulations to the Canadian curling teams for their medals), one commercial came up very frequently: an [Instacart ad](https://youtu.be/a1ysUdRLjaE?si=cZnSb7kU_4rOFX7f&t=13). This ad features Benson Boone and Ben Stiller, both big name celebrities, and was also directed by Academy Award winning director Spike Jonze. Given its talent and the fact it was aired during the Winter Olympics this advert likely cost a lot of money, and had one main call to action: Instacart now lets customers choose produce such as bananas directly, instead of the Instacart shopper choosing for them. All things considered, this new feature - allowing customers to choose their own bananas - probably cost Instacart millions of dollars.

Meanwhile, selecting your own produce at a grocery store is simply _the way most produce is sold_. Call this a "feature" of grocery shopping in person is an insult to feature engineering. Customers have bought bananas this way since bananas have been sold in stores.

In a different part of the software world, a director in its AI division at Meta installed the popular AI personal assistant OpenClaw on her work computer. This of course makes sense for a higher level employee at a top tech company, and doubly so for someone who works with AI solutions on a daily basis. However, after asking OpenClaw to delete some emails older than a given date, OpenClaw went a step too far and [deleted her entire inbox](https://www.businessinsider.com/meta-ai-alignment-director-openclaw-email-deletion-2026-2?op=1). Interestingly, the director did try to stop OpenClaw using prompts during the deletion process, but apparently OpenClaw just went ahead and deleted everything anyway. Again, this happened to a director at Meta.

These two stories tell me that even at high level tech companies, both internally and externally there is something deeply wrong with product quality in software. Mainly, there is a major lack of it.

An app that lets you grocery shop remotely and have your groceries delivered may have wanted to think about how customers choose fresh products earlier on. For years I heard friends talk about how much they enjoyed delivery platforms for groceries, but how selecting produce was a major sore spot. Buying packaged items for baking, for example, was excellent. Choosing the "right" banana, not so much. I do understand this is a complex problem - what constitutes a "good" cantaloupe? what if there are none in stock? - but there is something troubling about a company needing several millions of dollars and significant investments to solve this problem. And this is before even evaluating how well this new feature works in practice. Is there a problem here?

Meanwhile, the OpenClaw Meta director debacle shows that sometimes software solutions are simply bad. A computing assistant with access to large parts of your personal computer should not be able to make changes willy nilly, particularly after such actions were supposed to be canceled. There are some obvious user experience design failures here, but some bigger questions around benefit-cost ratios for using some kinds of software in the first place. If I use a piece of software that could help me with productivity but could have far reaching and terrible consequences, is there a problem here?

I think these stories are leading indicators for a bigger issue the software industry faces: how software quality may be dropping to levels where even business decisions are at risk. At some point, customers will actually avoid using (and paying for!) software of sufficiently low product quality. It may also be possible that entire categories of software are ditched due to a lack of quality, or necessity. Using the Instacart example, if enough people actually want to select their own items in a grocery shop, the will stop using software to do so. AI assistants may also go the way of the dodo if they are sufficiently dangerous.

Good software often is a method of solving a problem, done in a way that is effective or even enjoyable. Product quality is a vital part of this process, and software professionals should taking note that quality is not optional.
