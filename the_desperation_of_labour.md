One of the greatest artists of the Twentieth Century, Agnes Martin was mysterious but also incredibly talented. From the 1960s on, Martin focused almost completely on grid and stripe forms in her artwork. She painted tight and perfectly aligned grids while living in New York City in the 1960s, and after a hiatus from art, returned to making grid forms in the beloved work [On A Clear Day](https://www.gallery.ca/collection/artwork/on-a-clear-day).

<images of her art>

Martin's grid work appears simple, even easy. However, painting such tight lines creating a grid of small rectangles was a labour intensive process. Each vertical line was parallel to every other vertical line, and the same with horizontal lines. Graphite pencil scored through oil or acrylic created these lines, and in turn generated the grids we see. Martin was also known to destroy paintings that she felt were not quite right, instead of simply painting over the canvas. In a way, this process of destroying drafts increased Martin's workload.

Pieces such as [White Stone](https://www.guggenheim.org/artwork/2804) show narrow grids with nominal colouring, providing an almost serene appearance despite requiring much effort. This approach would continue for the rest of Martin's career, even as she left a two-dimensional grid pattern for horizontal -- and in rare cases, vertical -- bands on a square background. Maintaining perfectly parallel linear forms while applying colour necessitates focus and, in turn, labour. In many ways, one feels the work Martin put into her artwork upon careful inspection. One of the reasons Martin moved away from two-dimensional grids to lone-dimensional bands may be that constructing such grids was too labourious even for her. It takes a lot of work to get something precise to be perfect. 

< white stone >

Overall Martin was a determined individual. Even before leaving New York City to live in a highly self sufficient manner in the dessert of New Mexico, Martin was known to have a competitive streak. Being a fine artist was a lifelong goal, and even when Martin credibly achieved this, she continued living alone and producing art in the dessert. She was totally committed to a life of art. Hard work defined Martin, and thus she worked hard for most of her life.

Other artists have taken inspiration from Agnes Martin, taking up artistic practices that are premised on labour intensive work. Susan York [has constructed sculptures of graphite](https://www.susanyork.com/graphite-sculpture), requiring dedication and focus. Tammi Campbell [creates conceptual pieces](https://www.blouin-division.com/en/artists/tammi-campbell) calling back other artists and pieces in a manner that is labour intensive. These artists, like Martin, do not simply "make" art but purposely choose projects for themselves that are labourious. Art like this is the very opposite of the term "low-effort". Great art doesn't necessitate extensive labour but may well benefit from it, and depending on the artist, labourious projects may provide inspiration because of the extensive work placed on creation.

## Microsoft's Vibe Working - The Opposite of Labour

Recently, in one of a long series of artificial intelligence integrations into existing products, Microsoft announced it will add [ AI tooling into Office](https://www.theverge.com/news/787076/microsoft-office-agent-mode-office-agent-anthropic-models). This includes using AI in Excel, Word and PowerPoint, which are workhorses of the corporate world. Microsoft Office products are used by everyone at all levels of white collar work, from accounting clerks fresh out of school to the most seasoned of executives.

One of the goals of adding such tooling to Office is to allow for faster creation of Office-based assets with less effort. Instead of creating charts and images from data directly, users can write a single prompt to generate these items. Of course this also means that data creation, manipulation and text content can also be seemingly generated by a single prompt. In turn, this could mean that entire suites of for presentations - a slide deck containing charts generated from data, with explanations - can be created from typing a sentence or two. Such innovation could dramatically reduce the labour needed for corporate work.

There is, however, a trade-off. The technology that powers AIs is not perfect and does make mistakes. Since the output of these tools are based on statistical models, they produced "answer-shaped" results instead of hard calculations. As an example, a yearly forecast for a sizable business that depends on data generated by this AI-powered office suite could contains invalid data, or graphs that don't really reflect the reality of the data. Is there a problem here?

The scenario I come back to is the notion of the lazy executive. Instead of taking the time and effort to check and double check his work, he might simply get the machine to create something that looks basically right. Even worse is the scenario where this executive wholesale fabricates data and visualization, effectively outsourcing their primary function - making business decisions - to a machine. At the scale of Microsoft Office, this could be a big problem; someone will consider doing this in some way.

I think these scenarios also invite the question of the value of this work. If a machine can effectively do the work of a six-figure executive, what is the importance of this labour? How does that importance change if the output doesn't need to be correct or verified? Who is this work for?

These may seem like overly dramatic questions to ask, but I think it is important. There are serious downstream consequences for many high level business decisions. When staff are laid off, budgets cut, and lines of business shuttered, that affects people and business interests. One of the justifications for high executive compensation is that they make tough decisions with complex implications. When one of the key tools needed to figure out such decisions now produce fast but wrong answers, what does this mean for the whole concept of the enterprise?

I am confident most people will use these newly created artificial intelligence tools with best intentions. But for those who do not and do not plan to, I have to ask the question of what exactly they think they are doing? How critical is this labour?

In the case of the work done by artists like Agnes Martin, the criticality of the work is clear: these artists _need_ this work to exist in the world. These pieces are labourious but there is an urgency to them. I do believe the best artists find urgency in their art, almost requiring that they make the art that they do. This is where generative AI can fail: there is no urgency in work that never needed to really exist in the first place.
